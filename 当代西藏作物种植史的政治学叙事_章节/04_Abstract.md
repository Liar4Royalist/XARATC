## Abstract

This dissertation examines agricultural technological change in the modernization of the Tibet Autonomous Region (Xizang Autonomous Region, XAR), focusing on changes in highland barley and wheat varieties from the 1970s to the 1990s. Drawing on archival materials, oral histories, and fieldwork, it assesses the achievements and shortcomings of the People's Republic of China's governance of Tibet through the history of agricultural change. Its central theoretical question is: What factors and mechanisms determine agricultural technological change in XAR? The dissertation argues that the distribution of power between the state and farmers, together with their interactions, determines the outcomes of technological change and reshapes the production of knowledge, including farmers' agricultural culture and the state's understanding of agricultural development. In the process examined here, development became embedded in tradition, enabling the two to thrive together.

The study identifies two principal actors in agricultural technological change: the state and farmers. Their needs differ. The state generally promotes new technologies to increase agricultural output, whereas farmers face more complex constraints rooted in local livelihood systems. Farmers give priority to sustaining their livelihoods, and higher crop yields may undermine other essential components of those livelihoods. These divergent interests can lead the state and farmers to favor different directions of technological change. When translated into action, such differences generate conflicts whose outcomes depend on the power each actor holds and on their interactions.

Apart from the introduction and conclusion, the dissertation consists of four chapters. Chapter One uses ethnographic methods to explain the constraints shaping farmers' technological choices, examining the relationships among the natural environment, livelihood systems, and agricultural culture. Building on this analysis, Chapters Two, Three, and Four examine successive historical phases to explain how changes in the distribution of power between the state and farmers, and in their interactions, shaped agricultural technological change in XAR.

Chapter Two analyzes the promotion of winter wheat in the 1970s, showing how the state used political campaigns to advance technological change while retaining control over land use. Chapter Three examines the policy, introduced around 1980, of reducing winter wheat cultivation and expanding highland barley cultivation, and assesses its consequences. Chapter Four examines the period from the late 1980s through the 1990s, after agricultural production decisions had shifted to households. It shows how farmers' actions redirected the state's preference for short-stemmed varieties toward the taller varieties required by their livelihood systems. The dissertation closes with a brief conclusion.

Keywords: power; technological change; knowledge; development; tradition; highland barley; wheat
